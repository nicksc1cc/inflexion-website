The marketing team is being redesigned around AI agents.

Google and Amazon now automate much of the media work that marketing budgets used to pay people to do, and the largest agencies have cut thousands of roles in response. The same shift is changing the roles inside marketing teams and the relationship between brands and their agencies. Marketing teams are likely to be rebuilt around seven roles, with work divided differently between brands, agencies and platforms. Scored against those roles, most of today's 62 marketing roles are about halfway there.

01 / Platforms and agencies

Media buying and campaign setup are becoming platform settings.

Since 1 September 2026, Google has been moving search campaigns that use broad match or automatically created assets onto AI Max. The system chooses which searches an ad appears against and writes much of the ad copy itself. Amazon's Full-Funnel Campaigns ask the advertiser for a budget, a product list and creative, then decide the channels and audiences without letting the advertiser select either.

For more than a decade, setting up, bidding on and optimising campaigns like these was skilled work for planners, buyers and campaign managers. As more of it moves inside the platforms, the agencies that employed those people have reduced headcount. WPP has removed around 11,000 roles since early 2025 and has said most of its £500 million savings target will come from people costs. Among UK creative agencies counted in the IPA census, headcount fell 14.3% in the year to September 2025, and 30% of those agencies expect to make further cuts because of AI within a year.

Marketing teams inside brands have changed less so far, partly because much of their execution work sits with agencies and platforms rather than on their own payroll. That protection is weakening. BCG estimates that three to five people working alongside AI agents can run campaigns with 60% fewer resources than a conventional campaign team, and figures like that reach finance teams quickly. A marketing department with a large execution workload will be asked how it plans to change.

02 / The cost of cutting first

Reducing headcount first loses knowledge that is expensive to replace.

The quickest response to automation is to reduce headcount and let the platforms carry the work, and the saving appears in the next set of accounts. What leaves with those people is harder to measure. They know the brand's customers, why particular campaigns worked, which products carry the margin and how decisions get made internally. Agents produce good work when someone gives them that context. Without it they produce plausible work that gradually drifts away from what the business needs.

Most large employers say they intend to retrain staff before replacing them. In the World Economic Forum's 2025 survey, 85% of employers planned to prioritise upskilling and half planned to move staff from declining roles into growing ones. The same report expects 39% of workers' existing skills to be transformed or become outdated by 2030.

Retraining a marketing team for the roles described below takes around two years, based on published course lengths and a year of supervised project work. A company that starts in 2026 can do most of it with the people it already employs. A company that starts later is more likely to be cutting and hiring at the same time, paying a premium for skills that are still scarce, after the people with the most context have already left.

04 / Operating model

Small pods run the work and a shared studio builds the tools.

Most marketing work is likely to be run by pods of three to five people, each working with AI agents, which matches the structure BCG describes. A Growth Partner leads each pod and is accountable for its commercial results. Builders, the engineers in this model, sit in a shared studio and move between pods, turning anything used more than once into a tool every pod can use. Each discipline also keeps a practice that sets standards and runs training, so a creative working in a commerce pod still develops alongside other creatives.

05 / Delivery

Each project runs through six steps and leaves a reusable tool behind.

A beauty brand losing sales because shoppers now ask AI assistants for recommendations is a typical case. The Growth Partner defines the commercial problem, and the Decision Scientist sets a target and decides how it will be measured. A Builder writes an agent that corrects and enriches the brand's product data. The Agentic Systems Designer runs that agent day to day while the visibility and commerce specialists decide what it should change. The Decision Scientist then measures the effect on sales, and where the contract allows, the method behind the agent is reused for the next brand with the same problem.

06 / Client and agency

The client and agency relationship is being repriced around results and ownership.

Most agency contracts still price work by time. A retainer funds a team of named people, a rate card sets what each grade costs, and procurement benchmarks those rates against other agencies. That works while the work scales with people. Once agents and platform automation carry much of the execution, the hours behind a campaign fall sharply while the value of the decisions around it stays the same, and a price based on time no longer describes what the client is buying.

Three forms of pricing are likely to replace it, often in the same contract. Strategy and measurement are bought as a smaller retainer for senior judgement. Agents and tools are bought as builds, with a separate fee if the agency also runs them. Specialist work is bought as short projects with defined results, which also gives procurement something more useful to compare than day rates.

Three likely models

None of the three suits every brand. Each fits different conditions and carries different risks, and many brands will use more than one across markets or channels.

Model 1

Brand runs, agency builds

How it worksThe brand employs the growth, decision science and systems design roles and runs its own agents. Agencies supply Builders and specialists on defined projects.

SuitsBrands with large and steady spend, several markets on one platform stack and the ability to hire scarce technical people.

RisksHiring is slow and expensive, capability can leave with a few people, and the brand carries the full burden of quality control.

Model 2

Agency runs a managed service

How it worksThe agency runs pods and agents for the brand under a fee linked to results or a subscription. The brand sets targets and keeps its customer knowledge and data.

SuitsBrands with lean marketing teams, many channels or markets, or no wish to build technical capability of their own.

RisksDependence on one agency, an agency measuring its own results, and tools that are hard to move if the relationship ends.

Model 3

Hybrid

How it worksThe brand runs its always on channels in house. The agency builds the tools, runs specialist areas and peak periods, and may provide independent measurement.

SuitsBrands moving from a traditional agency model that want control of core channels without hiring every specialism.

RisksAccountability can blur at the points where work passes between the two sides unless ownership of each activity and each tool is written down.

Where the tensions are

The agency pyramid is under the most pressure. Holding company margins have long depended on billing many junior staff under a few senior ones, and agents now do much of the junior work. WPP expects most of its £500 million savings to come from people costs. The same change removes the traditional training ground for future senior staff, so agencies will need other ways to develop people, such as the two year route set out in the appendix.

Ownership of what gets built is the least settled question. An agent trained on a brand's data and refined over a year of its campaigns is valuable to both sides. Agencies want to reuse the method for other clients, and brands want to keep what their data and budget paid for. Few contracts separate the two today. Terms covering who owns the reusable method, who owns agents and outputs specific to the client, and what can be reused for competitors are likely to become standard.

The platforms are now suppliers and competitors at once. Google and Amazon sell automated execution directly to brands and report on its performance themselves, and independent tests reported by PPC Land have already questioned how AI Max performs, particularly in campaigns with limited budgets. Judging spend across platforms and measuring results independently are areas where neither the platform nor the agency running the work is well placed to assess itself.

07 / Skills gap

Today's marketing roles hold about half of the skills the new roles need.

We profiled 62 current brand, marketing, advertising and creative roles and 61 destination roles across 86 skills, then scored every current role against every destination at mid level. The median current role holds 54% of what its closest AI era role requires. The easiest move is from CRM Manager to Personalisation Manager, at 93%. Media Buyer and Media Planner, the roles most exposed to platform automation, score 39% and 41% against their closest AI era roles, which puts them among the furthest from the new work.

08 / What is still uncertain

Three parts of this analysis could prove wrong.

Platform automation may not perform as well as its adoption suggests. Independent testing of tools such as AI Max is still limited, and brands with small budgets or specialist products may find that manual control still pays. If that holds, more execution work stays with people than this analysis assumes.

Brands may move more slowly than agencies. Much of their execution work already sits outside their payroll, budgets are set annually, and few have redesigned a marketing function around AI agents before. The two year route assumes a decision to start, which many organisations have not yet made.

The seven roles are a reading of today's tools. Agent reliability, regulation such as the EU AI Act and attitudes to AI generated work could each move tasks back to people or remove roles kept here. The skill match scores compare role profiles rather than assessed individuals, so any real team will vary around them.

09 / Open questions

Seven questions that shape how a marketing team changes.

A leadership team would need answers to these before agreeing a marketing budget for 2027.

  1. Which parts of the marketing work are now done by platform automation, or soon could be, and what are the people who did that work doing instead?
  2. What will the marketing team look like in 2028, and who is accountable for getting it there?
  3. How many current staff could move into the new roles, and what happens for those who cannot?
  4. How much agency spend still pays for execution the agency is now automating, and do the contracts reflect that?
  5. Who in the team can direct AI agents today, and who checks the quality of what those agents produce?
  6. Which activities should stay in house, and who owns the agents and tools an agency builds on the brand's data?
  7. Which commercial results will the redesigned team be measured on?

A3 / Pod mix

The specialists in a pod depend on the problem it is solving.

Every pod has a Growth Partner and a Decision Scientist, and the rest changes with the work. A retailer selling through Amazon and AI shopping assistants needs commerce and visibility specialists. A consumer brand producing large volumes of content needs someone running creative systems. A company building its own AI tools needs Builders working alongside its engineers.

A4 / Training route

The route takes two years at half a day a week.

Everyone starts with the same four month foundation. People then spend eight months on courses and supervised work for their new role, and at month twelve they deliver one real piece of work in it before taking the title. The second year is spent in the role on live projects. That comes to about 340 hours per person, roughly 100 on courses and 240 on project work. Course hours come from published course lengths, and the totals are estimates.

A5 / Assessment

Assessment should weight evidence by how reliable it is.

Placing people on the right route depends on an accurate view of what they can already do. Tests and work samples are the most reliable evidence, an AI led interview and CV analysis less so, and a self rating least of all. Where sources disagree, lowering confidence in that skill is safer than averaging the scores, because it stops a self rating alone from placing someone on a route. Tracking how placed people perform over time shows whether the weighting is right.
