01. The shift

Two gatekeepers. Both algorithms.

A shopper wakes up, searches for a moisturiser, gets an AI answer that recommends three brands. She clicks one, checks reviews on social, then lands on a retailer page where an algorithm decides which product gets her attention. She never once made an independent choice.

Buyers used to find brands through search results and on the shelf. Both are now run by AI — the answer engines that pick who gets recommended, and the retail-media algorithms that pick who gets seen. The brand that loses either step becomes invisible. Twenty years of category-building disappears into a prompt you never saw.

02. Why beauty moves first

Every shopping agent starts with beauty.

Beauty queries map to structured attributes — skin type, shade, ingredient, concern — in a way other categories simply don't. When Google, OpenAI and Perplexity build their shopping agents, beauty is the category they test first, because it's the one the models can actually reason over. A foundation shade is a data point. A skincare concern is a filter. A sunscreen SPF is a number.

That makes beauty the proving ground for agentic commerce. Not a hypothesis. It's already live — Ulta on Universal Commerce Protocol, Sephora across AI Mode retailer rosters, Glossier on ChatGPT's agentic checkout. What works here gets copied everywhere else.

Stage 01

Win before the prompt is typed.

In the messy middle — that long stretch between "I wonder" and "I want" — brands are won or lost before purchase intent even exists. If a brand isn't front of mind, it isn't in the prompt. An AI agent can only recommend what it already knows. Twenty years of brand equity compresses into a single answer.

The Ebbinghaus forgetting curve is not a theory; it is a media planning constraint. Forty-eight percent of a new memory is gone within 24 hours without reinforcement. The brands that stay visible are the brands that stay consistent — not one burst, but a structured cadence. Spaced repetition is what makes a brand stick, not a single heavy week.

Stage 02

Feed the machines the data they actually need.

AI agents read structured data, not landing pages. A beautifully designed product page is invisible to a shopping agent if its attributes — ingredient, shade, concern, skin type — are not machine-readable. The product feed is now the primary user interface for AI commerce.

Google's Shopping Graph contains sixty billion products. When a user asks Gemini for a "hydrating serum for sensitive skin," the answer is assembled from product feeds — not brand copy. If your product is not tagged for "sensitive skin," it does not exist in the answer. This is not an SEO problem. This is a data problem.

60bn+
Products sit in Google's Shopping Graph, feeding AI Mode and the Universal Cart. Yet there is only 17–38% overlap between top organic rankings and AI citations — down from ~76% in 2024. The rules changed faster than the industry adapted.

Stage 03

The pause before the purchase.

Even a confident AI recommendation does not close the sale. Shoppers pause — checking reviews, watching a creator's routine, comparing on the retailer's site — before buying. That pause is a media moment. It is where paid media, retail media and creators earn the sale that the AI started. The recommendation gets them to the door. What they find when they arrive determines whether they buy.

Eighty-nine percent of AI-assisted shoppers double-check the recommendation before buying. Only 46% fully trust the AI on its own. The remaining 54% is Inflexion's opening — reinforcing the AI's answer at the exact moment a shopper is deciding whether to believe it. That is the most valuable media moment in the new funnel.

Stage 04

One shopper. One view. Seven surfaces.

Universal Cart and the Universal Commerce Protocol mean a shopper can discover on Search, decide in Gemini, and check out via a retailer — in one session. Attribution must follow them across every surface, not just the last click. The old model of separate attribution for paid, organic and retail was built for a world that no longer exists.

The brands that win this model are the ones that connect retail media, paid signals and organic data back to one customer view. Budget follows what is actually converting, not what is easiest to track. If you are running separate attribution for Amazon, for paid social and for brand health, you are seeing three different versions of the same customer.
